3: The Myth of Stress Relief & The Tight Shoe

You have been waiting all day to relax. So why does your jaw feel tighter after an hour of gaming than it did when you sat down?

Think about that for a moment. Really think about it.

You came home exhausted. Your shoulders were tight, your mind still replaying work conversations, deadlines, small frustrations. The day had drained you, and the only thing you wanted was to sit down, boot up the game, and let the stress melt away.

And for a few minutes, it worked. The loading screen appeared. The familiar music played. Your hands settled into their usual position on the keyboard or controller. A small wave of relief washed over you.

But what was that wave of relief, exactly?

Was it the game relaxing you? Or was it simply the feeling of stopping one activity and starting another? The cessation of work-related stress, not the presence of genuine relaxation?

If you had sat down at that same moment to do a jigsaw puzzle, read a book, or just stare out the window, you would have felt the same initial wave—the relief of sitting down, of stopping, of shifting your attention away from the demands of the day.

That relief is real. But it is not coming from the game. It is coming from the simple act of sitting down after a long day.

The game hijacks that relief and claims credit for it.

### The Tight Shoe

Let me tell you about a strange little magic trick. It is the trick that underpins this entire illusion, and once you see it, you will never be able to unsee it.

Imagine a man who puts on a pair of shoes two sizes too small every morning. All day long, his feet ache. Every step is uncomfortable. By evening, the pain is constant—a dull throb that colors everything he does.

Then he comes home, sits down, and takes off the shoes.

The relief is exquisite. His feet tingle. The pain drains away. For a few minutes, he feels better than he has felt all day. He closes his eyes and sighs, thinking: *This is the best part of my day. This is how I relax.*

But here is the question: who created the pain in the first place?

The man did—or rather, the tight shoes did. The relief he feels is not a gift. It is simply the temporary removal of a discomfort he imposed on himself hours earlier.

Now, here is the magic trick.

What if the man forgot that he chose the shoes? What if he believed the shoes were comfortable—his favorite pair, the only shoes he could wear? And what if, every evening, he felt that wave of relief and thought: *Thank God for these shoes. They relax me so much.*

That is exactly what the game does to you.

The game is the tight shoe.

It creates the agitation—the adrenaline spikes, the cognitive strain, the sensory overstimulation, the frustration of a lost match, the tension of an unpausable boss fight. And then, when the session ends, it takes the agitation away and calls it relaxation.

But the relaxation is just the removal of a tension the game itself created.

You did not need the game to relax. You needed the game to stop.

### The Body's Real Response

You might be thinking: *But I do feel better after gaming. My stress is lower. That proves it works.*

Let's look at what is actually happening in your body during and after a typical session.

Mid-match, your heart rate rises. Your breathing becomes shallow. Your shoulders hunch toward the screen. Your jaw clenches. Your pupils dilate. Adrenaline and cortisol—the body's primary stress hormones—circulate through your bloodstream. You are not aware of any of this because you are fully absorbed in the game, but your nervous system is in a state of acute arousal.

Then the session ends.

The adrenaline fades. The tension in your muscles slowly releases. Your breathing deepens. And you interpret this as relaxation. You tell yourself: *I feel better now. The game helped me unwind.*

But what you are actually feeling is the exhausted aftermath of sensory overstimulation. The body coming down from a stress spike. The same feeling you would get after a near-miss car accident or a heated argument—not relaxation, but the dull exhaustion of a nervous system recovering from a jolt.

If I told you I felt relaxed after drinking three espressos, would you believe me? Or would you look at my shaking hands and racing heart and see the truth?

Your body is telling you the truth right now. Are you listening?

Dry eyes. Clenched jaw. Tight neck. Shallow breathing. A faint buzz of adrenaline still circulating an hour after you logged off. That is not relaxation—that is the aftermath of stress.

Real relaxation does not leave you feeling drained. It leaves you feeling replenished.

### The Salt Water of Rest

Maybe you are willing to concede the point. Maybe you are thinking: *Okay, fine. It's not perfect relaxation. But it's still better than nothing. At least it takes my mind off work.*

Let me offer you a different frame.

Imagine you are thirsty. Genuinely, deeply thirsty. Your throat is dry, your head is foggy, and all you want is a glass of water.

Someone hands you a glass of salt water.

You drink it. For a moment, the liquid soothes your throat. But a few minutes later, your thirst is worse. The salt has dehydrated you further. What felt like relief was actually making the problem worse.

Would you say, *"At least it's wet"*?

Of course not. You would recognize that the salt water was counterfeit. It masqueraded as water, but it did the opposite of what water should do.

Gaming is the salt water of rest.

Your exhausted nervous system is thirsty for genuine replenishment: quiet, stillness, deep breathing, natural light, physical movement, real human connection. These are the things that actually restore a depleted body and mind.

But the game offers you the opposite. It floods your senses with stimulation. It spikes your adrenaline. It demands rapid decision-making and emotional engagement. It feels like it should work—it feels like a reward—but it only deepens the real hunger.

And here is the cruelest part: the more salt water you drink, the thirstier you become. The more you rely on gaming to unwind, the more wound-up you are. The more overstimulated your nervous system becomes, the more stimulation you need just to feel normal.

The flatline of everyday life—the boredom, the restlessness, the feeling that nothing else is engaging—is not a flaw in reality. It is a symptom of the overstimulation the game has been feeding you.

You have been drinking salt water for so long that plain water tastes boring.

### The Truth About Decompression

Let me be clear about something. Your need to decompress after a stressful day is real. It is legitimate. It is not a weakness, and it is not something to be ashamed of.

The human nervous system is not designed to run at full throttle for eight, ten, twelve hours straight. You need rest. You need recovery. You need time to let your body and mind settle back to baseline.

The question is not whether you need to decompress. The question is whether the game is actually helping you do it.

And the answer, as you can now see, is no.

The game is not decompressing you. It is re-compressing you. It is adding a new layer of stress on top of the stress you already carried home from work. It is spiking your adrenaline, depleting your dopamine, and exhausting your attention—all while telling you that this is what relaxation feels like.

Real decompression looks different.

It looks like a walk outside in the fading evening light. A quiet cup of tea while sitting on the couch. A conversation with someone you care about. Stretching out on the floor and feeling your muscles slowly unclench. Reading a book, taking a nap, or just sitting in silence and letting your mind wander.

These things do not spike your adrenaline. They do not demand rapid decision-making. They do not leave you feeling wired and hollow at the same time.

They replenish you.

But the game has convinced you that these things are boring. That they are not enough. That only the bright lights and rapid rewards of the screen can truly take the edge off.

That is the Big Monster whispering in your ear. And it is lying.

### The Exhale That Was Never Real

Let me bring you back to the moment you sat down tonight.

You were tired. Your mind was full. You booted up the game and felt that small wave of relief wash over you.

And now, an hour later, you are still sitting there. The screen is glowing. Your jaw is tight. Your eyes are dry. Your shoulders are hunched. You feel the same exhaustion you felt when you sat down—plus a new layer of mental fog and physical tension.

The game did not help you relax. It gave you something to be tense about, then took the tension away and called it relief.

That is the magic trick.

That is the tight shoe.

And now that you can see it, you can never unsee it.

The next time you sit down to game after a long day, ask yourself one question: *Am I relaxing right now? Or am I just too stimulated to notice I'm not?*

You do not need to stop playing to ask that question. You just need to notice.
